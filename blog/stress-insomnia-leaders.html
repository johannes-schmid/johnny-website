Why You Can't Sleep
And What Your Body
Is Actually Telling You

By Johnny Durán

← Back to journal

You can't sleep from stress. You know this. It's 3am, your mind is running a full board meeting without you, and the harder you try to shut it down, the louder it gets. You've Googled "sleep problems high performers" enough times to know you're not alone. What you haven't found yet is an honest explanation for what's actually happening — and why the standard advice keeps failing you.

I work with founders and executives across Europe, and the stress insomnia pattern is one of the most consistent things I encounter. It's also one of the most misdiagnosed. Because what most people are dealing with isn't a sleep problem. It's a nervous system problem that shows up at night — because that's the only time you've stopped performing long enough for it to surface.

The 3am Club: What's Actually Happening

Here's the physiology, without the textbook version. When you're under chronic stress, your body runs elevated cortisol — the hormone that, among other things, keeps you alert and ready to respond to threats. The problem is that modern high-performance stress isn't a lion chasing you. It's a calendar that never clears, a team that needs things, a business with a thousand open loops. The cortisol has nowhere to go. So at night, when the cognitive busyness slows, the body's arousal system is still running.

Waking between 2am and 4am is particularly common in this pattern — it corresponds to a natural cortisol rise that, in a regulated nervous system, happens gently and helps you transition back into sleep. In a chronically stressed system, that rise is exaggerated. Sleep problems from stress aren't about not being tired enough. You're exhausted. The system just can't switch off.

"Your body isn't broken. It's doing exactly what a body does when it's been running on survival for too long."

The Performance Cost Nobody Talks About Honestly

Sleep deprivation in high-performers is often normalised as a badge of commitment. What it actually does, according to research from sleep scientist Matthew Walker and his team, is significantly impair cognitive function even after just one or two nights of disrupted sleep. For leaders specifically, the cost shows up in:

  • Decision quality. Sleep-deprived brains are measurably worse at risk assessment and favour short-term, impulsive choices over considered strategy.
  • Emotional regulation. The amygdala — your threat-response centre — becomes overactive. Everything feels slightly more dangerous, more urgent, more personal than it actually is.
  • Creative thinking. Deep problem-solving and pattern recognition both depend on consolidated sleep. Without it, you're working with a significantly reduced toolkit.
  • Physical recovery. Stress hormones that aren't metabolised during sleep accumulate. The tension you're carrying in your body has nowhere to discharge.

None of this is news to you intellectually. What's useful is understanding that this isn't a discipline problem. You're not failing to sleep because you're not trying hard enough. You're failing to sleep because the system driving wakefulness hasn't been addressed.

The problem isn't
that you can't sleep.
It's what's keeping you awake.

Why Sleep Hygiene Advice Doesn't Work for You

No screen time before bed. Consistent sleep schedule. Cool room. No caffeine after 2pm. You've heard all of it. Some of you have tried all of it. And it helps — marginally. Because sleep hygiene addresses the conditions for sleep. It doesn't address the state your nervous system is in when it tries to sleep.

Think of it this way: if you've been running a car engine at high revs all day, you can't just turn off the ignition and expect the engine to cool instantly. There's heat, pressure, momentum that needs to dissipate. Your nervous system works the same way. The protocol of how you sleep matters far less than the state you're in before you try to sleep — and that state is determined by what's happened to your stress response across the entire day.

3 Things That Actually Address the Source

1. Discharge the day before you try to close it

This is not meditation (though that helps). This is about giving your nervous system a specific signal that the threat-response is no longer required. Physical movement — a walk, light exercise, breathwork — metabolises the cortisol and adrenaline that have built up during the day. This is the step most people skip entirely, going from work straight to screen straight to bed and wondering why their brain won't cooperate.

2. Close your open loops before you lie down

The mind runs at night on unresolved commitments. A brief five-minute capture practice — write down everything that's on your mental stack, what needs doing tomorrow, what you're worried about — externalises the cognitive load. Your brain stops rehearsing because it has evidence the information is safe. This is simpler and more effective than most people expect.

3. Address the chronic stress, not just the nights

This is the work nobody wants to hear, but it's the only one that produces lasting change. If your nervous system is running in a chronic stress pattern — if your baseline is elevated, if rest doesn't feel restorative even when you get it — then you need to address that pattern directly. How you sleep better under pressure isn't ultimately a sleep question. It's a question about what level of pressure your system is carrying, and whether that's sustainable.
